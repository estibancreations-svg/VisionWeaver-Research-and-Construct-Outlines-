Script Draft - Verbatim

The script is the source - these words do not change in production

Speaker is Aelune throughout, except the student's quoted lines inside the Story. She defines before she comforts. Pauses after definitions and punch lines are part of the script - see the standards mapping.

Hook

"By 'too small, too old, too late' — define your terms."

Story - three verdicts, three stones

A student came to my shelf with three verdicts, worn like a verdicts are worn — close to the chest, where no one can argue with them. "I am too small for the work," she said. "Too old to begin. Too late to matter." I did not comfort her first. Comfort, offered before definition, only polishes the verdict. I set out three stones on the table: a pebble, a river stone, and a boulder. "Which is strong?" I asked. She pointed at the boulder, as anyone would. "Strong for what?" I asked. "To hold a door? The pebble holds a page against the wind. To cross a river? The river stone is the step. To be carried on a long road? The pebble travels; the boulder stays home."

Delivery note. No comfort before definition - that order is the teaching. Let each stone answer its own question: the pebble, the river stone, and the boulder each do different work.

Script Draft - Verbatim, Continued

Three words that were never one word

Then I defined the three words she had been using as if they were one word. Size: how much space a thing takes. Strength: whether a thing does the work it was made for. Worth: whether a life is lived honestly, in its own measure. And these were never the same word. I showed her, the way the night shows things: the smallest lamp in my observatory guides the whole night's charts. The oldest star chart on my shelf is the most trusted, because it has been checked against the most skies. The late arrival — a moth at dusk — pollinates what the day insects missed, because it comes when the flowers open at night. Small did the work. Old held the trust. Late arrived on time.

Delivery notes for this section.
Pause after each definition - Size, Strength, Worth - so the three words separate cleanly.
"Small did the work. Old held the trust. Late arrived on time." lands as three short lines, each followed by a pause.

Script Draft - Verbatim, Continued

Verdicts wearing a ruler's clothes

Reframe

Hear me, plainly. Appearance measures appearance. Nothing else has ever been measured by it. "Too small, too old, too late" are not measurements. They are verdicts wearing a ruler's clothes. Take off the costume. Define, then decide. When you define size, strength, and worth separately, the verdict falls apart — because it was never one judgment. It was three confusions, stacked, wearing one voice. And a confusion, once named, cannot sentence you.

Delivery note. "Appearance measures appearance" is the aphorism of the episode - it also carries the Short. Hold a full pause after it, and after "a confusion, once named, cannot sentence you."

Script Draft - Verbatim, Continued

Do the smallest version, today

Practice

So here is the demonstration rule. Do not argue with the doubt. Do the smallest version of the thing, today, and let the doing answer. One page. One call. One step. Not the whole work — the smallest version that is still truly the work. Evidence beats argument, every time, because argument happens in your head, and evidence happens in the world. Do it small, and watch what the world does back.

Payoff / Bridge

Evidence beats argument. And some evidence only arrives in the dark — the lamp, the chart, the moth — things you cannot see until the day's bright verdicts dim. Which raises the next question, the one about knowing: when the evidence is in, how will you know which call is right? That is next week's teaching: how you will know, and the calm test that tells you.

Where this sits in the episode. End of script draft. The payoff sits at 70-85% of runtime in the scene map, and the bridge - not a hard goodbye - carries the viewer to Speech 7, "How You Will Know."
