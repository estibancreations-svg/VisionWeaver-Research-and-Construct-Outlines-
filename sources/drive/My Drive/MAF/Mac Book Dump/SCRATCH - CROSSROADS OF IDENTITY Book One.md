Author Biography
Steven Henry is a Tampa-based author whose work explores themes of identity, community, and belonging within LGBTQ+ spaces. Drawing from personal experience and deep engagement with Tampa's queer history, Steven crafts stories that honor the past while imagining transformative futures. "Convergence" is his debut novel, the first in the Crossroads of Identity series. When not writing, Steven volunteers with local LGBTQ+ youth organizations and works to preserve the histories of safe spaces that have sheltered generations of queer people.
DEDICATION
To all who find their way home through chosen family, and to those who create spaces where belonging is possible.
SERIES OVERVIEW
The "Crossroads of Identity" series follows the evolution of a safe house network for LGBTQ+ youth, beginning with the discovery of a hidden legacy and expanding into a national movement for social change. Set primarily in Tampa, Florida, the series explores themes of family, identity, community, and the ongoing struggle for acceptance and equality.
Book One: "Convergence" tells the story of how disparate lives intersect around a mysterious inheritance, as four unlikely inheritors discover their interconnected roles in preserving a sanctuary that has sheltered generations of LGBTQ+ youth.

Chapter 1: The News (Marcus's Perspective)
The Westshore Financial District gleamed in the January sun, all glass and steel reflecting Tampa's cloudless blue sky. Marcus Reynolds straightened his burgundy tie—his power tie, the one he reserved for closing deals—and leaned forward across the polished conference table. His voice carried the precise balance of authority and reassurance that had made him one of the firm's rising stars at only thirty-two.
"What Mr. Alvarez is offering," Marcus said, gesturing to the music producer seated beside him, "is not just distribution, but a comprehensive marketing strategy tailored specifically to emerging artists from underrepresented backgrounds." He slid the contract toward the young rapper seated across from him, whose skeptical expression had barely shifted during the hour-long negotiation. "This clause here guarantees you retain creative control over your content while accessing resources that would typically require years to develop independently."
The rapper, Jayden King, exchanged glances with his manager before reaching for the contract. Marcus had spent weeks crafting these terms, understanding that for artists from Tampa's East Side, control was often more valuable than cash. He'd been that kid once—suspicious of suits, protective of his voice. Different context, same fear.
His phone vibrated against his thigh. Marcus ignored it. In negotiations, attention was currency, and he never cheapened himself by checking messages. The vibration came again, more insistent this time. Then again. Three texts in rapid succession—his emergency protocol with Carlos.
"Take your time reviewing Section 4," Marcus said smoothly, sliding his chair back slightly. "It outlines the royalty structure we discussed." He reached for his water glass with his left hand while his right discreetly retrieved his phone beneath the table.
The text wasn't from Carlos.
Marcus, it's Victor Mendoza. Elijah Johnson passed last night. Peacefully, in his sleep. Thought you should know.
The conference room suddenly seemed airless. Marcus's fingers tightened around his phone, the sleek case slippery against his suddenly damp palm. He forced his expression to remain neutral, his breathing even, as the words blurred before him.
Will reading scheduled for Friday at Brennan & Associates. Elijah left specific instructions for you to attend.
I'm sorry to deliver this news. He spoke of you often.
Marcus set his phone face-down on his thigh. He took a careful sip of water, the glass nearly slipping from his grasp. Across the table, Jayden was pointing to something in the contract, his manager leaning in with furrowed brows. Marcus needed to respond, to be present, to do his job. The job that had become his armor, his identity, his escape.
"That clause allows for renegotiation after the first million streams," he heard himself saying, his voice coming from somewhere distant. "It's unusually artist-friendly for a first contract."
Elijah was gone. The man who had opened his door to a terrified fifteen-year-old boy with nowhere else to go. The man who had shown Marcus what it meant to live with integrity when the world denied your right to exist. The man who knew Marcus—the real Marcus—better than anyone in this gleaming tower of glass and ambition.
"Mr. Reynolds?" Jayden's manager was looking at him expectantly. "We asked about the promotional timeline."
"Of course," Marcus said, sliding seamlessly back into his professional persona. "Mr. Alvarez has committed to a three-phase rollout beginning in March." He outlined the details with practiced precision, not a tremor in his voice, not a crack in his façade.
This was what Elijah had called his "compartmentalization superpower"—the ability to seal off parts of himself so completely that they might as well exist in parallel universes. Right now, Attorney Reynolds was handling a career-making contract negotiation while somewhere in another universe, a former scared kid named Marcus was crumbling under the weight of grief.
The meeting concluded with handshakes and signatures. Marcus escorted the clients to the elevator, exchanging the exact right amount of small talk—professional but personable, engaged but not overly familiar. His senior partner clapped him on the shoulder as they walked back to their offices.
"Nicely done, Reynolds. Alvarez has been trying to sign King for months. This puts us in a strong position with his whole label."
"Thank you, sir. I think it's a solid partnership for both parties." Marcus's voice sounded normal to his own ears, which seemed impossible.
"We're discussing partnership track at the executive committee meeting next month," his senior partner said, pausing at his office door. "Keep this up, and your name will be on the table."
Marcus nodded, his practiced smile firmly in place. "I appreciate that, sir."
He made it to his office, closed the door, and managed three steps toward his desk before the pressure in his chest became unbearable. The bathroom—he needed the privacy of his executive bathroom. His legs carried him there on autopilot, his hand fumbling with the lock before he collapsed against the marble vanity.
In the mirror, Attorney Reynolds still looked immaculate—crisp white shirt, burgundy tie, not a hair out of place in his close-cropped fade. But his eyes belonged to fifteen-year-old Marcus, wide with fear and loss.
"You can stay as long as you need to," Elijah had said, showing him to a small room on the third floor of the Victorian house in Ybor City. "There's just two rules here: respect yourself, and respect others. Everything else we can figure out as we go."
Marcus had stood frozen in the doorway, his backpack clutching a hastily packed collection of belongings—some clothes, his school books, the journal his grandmother had given him that his father hadn't found and burned along with the texts.
"I don't... I can't pay rent," he'd managed to say, his voice cracking with the strain of not crying.
Elijah's laugh had been warm, his hand gentle on Marcus's shoulder. "Child, this isn't a boarding house. This is a safe house. The only payment expected is that someday, when you're able, you'll help someone else who needs it."
A sob tore from Marcus's throat, raw and unfamiliar. He couldn't remember the last time he had cried. Years, certainly. He pressed his palms against the cool marble, trying to ground himself as the grief hit in waves. Elijah was gone. The one person who had seen him—truly seen him—when the world had turned its back.
His phone vibrated again. A text from Carlos: Lunch today? I can break free at 1.
Marcus stared at the message, his thumbs hovering over the screen. Carlos Vega, the architectural engineer he'd been seeing for the past three months. Casual, discreet, compartmentalized. Carlos knew he was an attorney specializing in entertainment law. He knew Marcus had grown up in Tampa, attended Florida State and then Howard Law. He knew Marcus preferred sushi to steak and bourbon to beer.
Carlos did not know about the safe house in Ybor City. He did not know about the deacon father and the Christian school teacher mother who had given their son an ultimatum when he was fifteen. He did not know about Elijah Johnson.
Can't today. Work emergency. Raincheck?
Marcus splashed cold water on his face, careful not to wet his shirt collar. He dried off with a paper towel, taking slow, deliberate breaths until his reflection showed Attorney Reynolds again—composed, professional, in control.
He had three more meetings today, a brief to review, and a dinner with potential clients. The will reading wasn't until Friday. He had three days to prepare himself, to reinforce the walls between his worlds, to figure out how to say goodbye to Elijah without letting the carefully constructed life he'd built come crashing down around him.
Marcus straightened his tie, unlocked the bathroom door, and stepped back into the life he had chosen—the life that existed as far as possible from a Victorian safe house in Ybor City and the scared boy who had once found refuge there.

The downtown condo was exactly twenty-three floors above the Tampa streets, high enough that the city lights blurred into a gentle glow through the floor-to-ceiling windows. Marcus stood at those windows now, bourbon in hand, still wearing his suit pants and dress shirt though he'd discarded the tie and jacket hours ago.
He'd made it through the day. The meetings, the brief, the client dinner—all handled with the precision and focus that had become his trademark. No one had noticed anything amiss, not even his assistant who prided herself on reading his moods.
Now, in the privacy of his condo, the grief he'd held at bay came rushing back. Not in sobs this time, but in a hollow ache that seemed to echo through the minimalist space. The condo had been featured in Tampa Bay Modern Living last year—"Rising Legal Star's Urban Oasis." The interviewer had praised its "clean lines and uncluttered aesthetic," never guessing that the lack of personal touches was deliberate, another form of compartmentalization.
Marcus moved from the window to the bedroom closet, setting his bourbon on the dresser. He pushed aside the precise rows of suits to reach the back corner, where a plain shoebox sat on the highest shelf. He hadn't opened it in years, had almost convinced himself he kept it out of simple sentimentality rather than as a lifeline to a self he rarely acknowledged.
The box contained the few items he'd salvaged from his parents' house: his grandmother's Bible with his birth date inscribed inside the cover; a photograph of him at age ten with his father at a father-son church retreat, both smiling in matching T-shirts; a debate team medal from ninth grade; and a small, carved wooden figure of a man reading a book.
Elijah had given him the carving the day Marcus left for college. "To remind you that knowledge is power," he'd said, "but wisdom is knowing how to use it."
Marcus ran his finger over the smooth wood, worn from years of handling during his college and law school days. He'd kept it on his desk then, a small act of courage in otherwise careful years. When had he decided it needed to be hidden away? When had he become so afraid of questions, of connections between his worlds?
His phone rang, startling him. His mother's name appeared on the screen.
Marcus stared at it in disbelief. They spoke perhaps three times a year—Christmas, his birthday, and Mother's Day. Brief, stilted conversations that never ventured beyond surface pleasantries. Had she somehow heard about Elijah? Did she even remember who Elijah was?
The call went to voicemail. Seconds later, a text appeared: Just checking in. Your father and I are praying for you.
Generic words that could mean everything or nothing. Marcus set the phone down without responding and returned the wooden figure to its box, which he then placed back on the high shelf, hidden behind the suits.
He retrieved his bourbon and moved to the kitchen, where he pulled a rarely-used pan from a cabinet. He wasn't hungry, but he needed to do something physical, something normal. An omelet, perhaps. He could manage that.
As he cracked eggs into a bowl, his mind drifted to the will reading on Friday. Why would Elijah have left specific instructions for him to attend? They had stayed in touch over the years, but distantly. A coffee every few months. Holiday cards. The occasional text when something in the news affected the LGBTQ+ community.
Marcus had visited the safe house less and less frequently as his career advanced, as the risk of someone recognizing him increased. His last visit had been nearly a year ago, after Florida's "Don't Say Gay" bill had passed. He'd found Elijah on the front porch, looking older than Marcus had ever seen him, a newspaper in his lap with the headline: "Florida Returns to Dark Ages with Anti-LGBTQ Legislation."
"Feels like 1977 all over again," Elijah had said, referring to the year Tampa Pride was banned. "Except I had more fight in me back then."
"This will be challenged in court," Marcus had assured him, speaking as Attorney Reynolds rather than as the boy Elijah had once sheltered. "There are solid constitutional arguments against it."
Elijah had given him a long look. "Laws matter, Marcus. But so do hearts. While you're fighting in courtrooms, who's fighting in living rooms? In classrooms? Who's telling these kids they deserve to exist when their own state is saying they don't?"
The memory stung. Marcus had made a substantial anonymous donation to a legal defense fund the next day, but he hadn't returned to the safe house since. Had Elijah been disappointed in him? Was the will reading some final attempt to pull Marcus back into a world he'd worked so hard to distance himself from?
The eggs were burning. Marcus cursed, moving the pan off the heat. The acrid smell filled the kitchen, another failure in a day marked by them. He dumped the blackened mess into the trash and leaned against the counter, suddenly exhausted.
Friday was only three days away, but it stretched before him like an eternity—an eternity of maintaining his composure while the foundations of his carefully constructed life shifted beneath his feet. Marcus Reynolds, Esquire, on the verge of partnership, respected by colleagues and clients alike, was about to come face to face with the past he'd spent seventeen years trying to outrun.
And Elijah—dear, wise, unflinching Elijah—wouldn't be there to help him through it this time.
