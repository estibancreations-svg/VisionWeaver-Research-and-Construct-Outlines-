# Chapter 7: The Son

*June 2024*

Alex Johnson adjusted his baseball cap in the rearview mirror of his mother's car, a nervous habit that had intensified since they'd begun visiting the safe house. At nineteen, he had grown more comfortable in his transgender identity over the past few years, but certain situations still triggered the old anxieties—the feeling of being scrutinized, evaluated, found wanting in some fundamental way.

Meeting people connected to the grandfather he had never known definitely qualified as one of those situations.

"You okay?" his mother asked from the driver's seat, her nurse's perception catching the subtle signs of his discomfort.

"Just processing," Alex replied, offering a small smile. "It's a lot, you know? Finding out about Elijah, visiting the place he created, meeting all these people who knew him when I never got the chance."

Katherine nodded, understanding in her eyes. "It is a lot. For both of us. But there's no pressure here, Alex. We can engage with this community at whatever pace feels right."

The reassurance was characteristic of his mother's approach—creating space for him to explore at his own pace, offering support without imposing expectations. It was this same approach that had made his coming out and transition relatively smooth compared to the experiences of many of his transgender peers. When he had first expressed his gender identity at fifteen, Katherine had educated herself, found appropriate resources, and advocated for him with the same quiet determination she brought to her nursing practice.

Now, as they approached the safe house for his third visit—this time specifically to attend the Thursday youth support group Jordan had invited him to join—Alex felt a mixture of anticipation and nervousness. The previous visits had been positive experiences; the exhibition opening had introduced him to several young people in the safe house's programs who shared aspects of his experience, and he had felt an immediate connection with Jordan, whose non-binary perspective offered valuable insight into gender identity beyond the binary.

But this visit felt more significant somehow—less about exploring his grandfather's legacy and more about potentially becoming part of the community Elijah had built. The youth support group represented a more sustained engagement, a regular commitment rather than a one-time visit.

"We're here," Katherine announced as she parked in front of the Victorian mansion that housed the safe house. The building was impressive—three stories of restored architectural details, with a wide porch and colorful garden that created an immediately welcoming impression.

Alex took a deep breath, centering himself before exiting the car. "You don't have to wait," he told his mother. "Jordan said the group usually runs about ninety minutes, and I can text you when it's wrapping up."

Katherine studied him for a moment, maternal concern balanced with respect for his independence. "You're sure? I don't mind waiting."

"I'm sure," he confirmed. "Maybe use the time to get coffee or something? I'll text when we're finishing up."

With a nod of acceptance, Katherine wished him luck, and Alex made his way up the path to the safe house entrance. The door opened before he could knock, revealing Jordan with their characteristic blue-purple hair and welcoming smile.

"Perfect timing," they greeted him. "We're just getting set up in the community room. A few of the regulars are already here, and a couple of new participants are expected."

Alex followed Jordan through the foyer and down a hallway decorated with artwork created by safe house residents and community members. The community room was a large, comfortable space with flexible seating arrangements, currently configured in a circle of chairs and couches that suggested conversation rather than presentation.

Several young people were already present, including two Alex had met during the exhibition opening—Zach, a transgender man in his early twenties who volunteered with the safe house's health clinic, and Mia, a lesbian high school senior who participated in the arts program. They greeted Alex with genuine warmth, making space for him in the circle.

As more participants arrived, Alex observed the diversity of the group—varying ages from mid-teens to early twenties, different racial and ethnic backgrounds, diverse gender identities and sexual orientations. What united them was not uniformity of experience but a shared commitment to creating space where authentic self-expression was valued over conformity to external expectations.

Jordan began the session with brief introductions, establishing the group's agreements around confidentiality and mutual respect before opening the discussion to whatever concerns or celebrations participants wanted to share. The format was intentionally flexible, allowing the conversation to flow according to the group's needs rather than following a predetermined agenda.

Initially, Alex found himself more comfortable listening than speaking, absorbing the dynamics of the group and the range of experiences being shared. A transgender girl discussed challenges with her school's bathroom policies; a non-binary youth celebrated receiving their first prescription for hormone therapy; a gay teenager sought advice about coming out to extended family members.

Throughout these varied discussions, Jordan facilitated with a light touch, offering guidance when needed but primarily creating space for peer support and shared wisdom to emerge from the group itself. Alex was impressed by their approach—the balance of structure and flexibility, the way they validated each person's experience while connecting it to broader patterns and possibilities.

About halfway through the session, during a natural pause in the conversation, Jordan turned to Alex with a gentle invitation: "Alex, would you like to share anything about your experience or ask any questions of the group? No pressure, but the space is open if you'd like."

The direct address caught Alex slightly off guard, but he found himself ready to engage rather than retreat. "Sure," he began, adjusting his cap again in that nervous gesture. "I guess I'm still processing a lot of things—not just being transgender, which I've had a few years to figure out, but this new connection to a grandfather I never knew existed until recently."

The mention of this family discovery drew interested looks from the group, creating an opening for Alex to share more of his story—about his mother learning of Elijah's identity after her own mother's death, about discovering his grandfather had been a significant figure in Tampa's LGBTQ+ community, about the DNA test confirming their biological connection.

"It's strange," he continued, finding his voice growing stronger as he articulated thoughts that had been developing since their first visit to the safe house. "Finding out that the grandfather I never knew created this place that might have been really helpful to me when I was first figuring out my gender identity. Like there's this connection across time that existed before I knew about it."

Jordan nodded, understanding in their eyes. "That's a profound observation, Alex. Many of us experience those kinds of connections across time in our LGBTQ+ journeys—discovering historical precedents for our experiences, finding community with elders who navigated similar challenges in different contexts, recognizing patterns that transcend individual lives."

The framing helped Alex articulate something that had been forming in his mind since learning about Elijah. "It makes me wonder about genetic components to identity," he said. "Not just gender identity, but this drive to create community, to make space for people to be themselves. My mom has always been like that—creating room for me to explore who I am without imposing expectations. And from what I've learned, that was central to Elijah's approach too."

This reflection opened a broader discussion about inheritance—what aspects of identity might have biological components, what came from family culture and modeling, what emerged from broader social context. Several group members shared their own experiences of recognizing family patterns across generations, of finding unexpected connections to relatives through shared traits or interests, of navigating the complex interplay of biology, psychology, and social environment in shaping identity.

Throughout this conversation, Alex found himself increasingly comfortable participating, his initial nervousness giving way to genuine engagement. The group created space for exploration without requiring definitive answers, for the expression of uncertainty and curiosity without judgment or simplification.

By the time the session concluded, Alex felt a sense of connection that had been missing in other contexts of his life. His friends at the community college where he was studying computer science were supportive of his transgender identity, but few shared that experience directly. The online communities he participated in offered valuable resources but lacked the immediacy of in-person interaction. This group, with its diverse perspectives and shared commitment to authentic self-expression, filled a gap he hadn't fully recognized until now.

As participants began to disperse, Jordan approached Alex with a question: "What did you think? Would you be interested in coming back next week?"

"Definitely," Alex replied without hesitation. "This was... exactly what I needed, actually. A space to process all these different aspects of identity and experience with people who get it."

Jordan's expression warmed with evident pleasure at this response. "I'm glad to hear that. The group seems to really connect with your perspective too—especially that insight about connections across time and generations. That's something we've been exploring in various safe house programs, this idea of lineage that transcends simple biology or legal definition."

The observation resonated with Alex's own thinking about his relationship to Elijah—a connection that existed outside direct interaction, that operated through shared values and commitments rather than personal relationship. "I've been thinking about that a lot," he admitted. "About what it means to be connected to someone I never met, whose DNA I carry but whose life I never shared."

"That's a profound question," Jordan acknowledged. "And one that many LGBTQ+ people grapple with in different ways—finding connection to historical figures who shared aspects of our experience, creating chosen family when biological family is unavailable or unsupportive, recognizing patterns and possibilities that transcend individual lives."

Their conversation was interrupted by Alex's phone buzzing with a text from his mother, asking if the session was concluding soon. He confirmed that it was wrapping up and that she could pick him up in about fifteen minutes.

"Before you go," Jordan said, "I wanted to mention that we're developing a digital storytelling project as part of the safe house's youth programs. Using video, audio, and interactive media to document diverse LGBTQ+ experiences across generations. Given your interest in both technology and intergenerational connection, it might be something you'd want to get involved with."

The invitation caught Alex's interest immediately. As a computer science student with a focus on interactive media, he had been looking for opportunities to apply his technical skills to meaningful projects. "That sounds really interesting," he said. "What kind of involvement are you thinking?"

"Whatever aligns with your interests and skills," Jordan replied. The project appealed to Alex on multiple levels—the opportunity to apply his technical skills to meaningful content, the chance to explore intergenerational LGBTQ+ experiences, the potential to connect with his grandfather's legacy through documenting the community he had helped build. "I'd definitely like to learn more," he told Jordan. "Maybe next time I'm here, you could show me what you've developed so far?"

As they walked together toward the foyer to wait for Katherine's arrival, Alex felt a growing sense of connection to the safe house community—not just as Elijah's grandson but as a participant in his own right, bringing his unique combination of experiences and skills to the ongoing work of creating space for authentic self-expression.

"I am," Alex acknowledged. "It's filling a gap I didn't fully recognize until now—connecting with other LGBTQ+ people across different ages and experiences, processing aspects of identity in a community context rather than just individually or online."

Katherine listened attentively, asking thoughtful questions that helped him clarify his thinking about next steps.
