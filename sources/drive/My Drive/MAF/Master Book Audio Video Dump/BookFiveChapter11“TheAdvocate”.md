## Chapter 11: "The Advocate" (Marcus's Perspective)
*July 2026*

Marcus Reynolds adjusted his tie as he waited in the anteroom of the Senate Judiciary Committee. The marble walls and ornate moldings of the Capitol building contrasted sharply with the community centers and church basements where he had begun his advocacy work a decade earlier. Yet here he was, preparing to testify before Congress about the Safe House Protection Act—legislation that would provide federal funding and legal protections for LGBTQ+ youth shelters nationwide.

"You ready for this?" Carlos asked quietly beside him, reaching over to straighten Marcus's already-perfect tie in a gesture that was more about connection than appearance.

Marcus nodded, drawing strength from his husband's presence. "As ready as I'll ever be. The data is solid, the stories are compelling, and the need is undeniable. Now it's just a matter of communicating that effectively."

Carlos squeezed his hand briefly. "You've got this. No one understands the legal landscape for these kids better than you do."

At forty-one, Marcus had established himself as one of the country's leading legal advocates for LGBTQ+ youth rights. What had begun as volunteer legal assistance at the Tampa safe house had evolved into a comprehensive advocacy initiative that combined direct representation, policy development, and strategic litigation. His work had helped shape state-level protections in Florida and had contributed to legal precedents that were now cited in cases across the country.

The committee aide appeared at the door. "Mr. Reynolds? They're ready for you now."

Marcus rose, gathering his materials and taking a deep breath. Carlos gave him an encouraging nod as he followed the aide into the hearing room. The space was intimidating—senators arrayed on a raised dais, staff members clustered behind them, cameras positioned to capture every moment for C-SPAN and news outlets. But Marcus had prepared meticulously for this moment, understanding its potential significance for the safe house network's future.

"Mr. Reynolds, please raise your right hand," the committee chair instructed. After Marcus was sworn in, the chair continued, "You may proceed with your opening statement."

Marcus adjusted the microphone and began speaking, his voice steady and clear. "Chairman Mitchell, Ranking Member Gonzalez, and distinguished members of the committee, thank you for the opportunity to testify today regarding the Safe House Protection Act. My name is Marcus Reynolds, and I serve as Legal Director for the National Safe House Network, which currently includes facilities in twenty-three states providing emergency shelter, transitional housing, and comprehensive support services for LGBTQ+ youth experiencing homelessness."

He continued, presenting statistics on LGBTQ+ youth homelessness, explaining the unique challenges these young people faced, and describing how the safe house model addressed these needs through a holistic approach to support and empowerment. Throughout his testimony, Marcus balanced data-driven analysis with humanizing stories, putting faces and experiences to the numbers without exploiting individual traumas for emotional effect.

"The legislation before you today would accomplish three critical objectives," Marcus explained. "First, it would establish federal funding for safe houses meeting evidence-based standards of care. Second, it would provide legal protections for these facilities against discriminatory zoning and licensing practices that have been used to prevent their operation in some jurisdictions. And third, it would create a national technical assistance center to support the development of new safe houses in underserved areas."

As he concluded his opening statement, Marcus felt a cautious optimism. The committee members had been attentive, several nodding at key points in his presentation. The bipartisan sponsorship of the bill reflected growing recognition across the political spectrum that addressing youth homelessness required specialized approaches for different populations.

The questioning began with Senator Mitchell, the committee chair. "Mr. Reynolds, some critics have suggested that specialized shelters for LGBTQ+ youth are unnecessary and potentially discriminatory against other homeless youth. How would you respond to that concern?"

Marcus had anticipated this question. "Senator, the evidence clearly shows that LGBTQ+ youth face unique challenges in traditional shelter settings, including harassment, discrimination, and even violence. Safe houses don't exclude other youth in need—many serve allies and siblings of LGBTQ+ residents. But they do create environments specifically designed to address the particular traumas and needs of young people who have often been rejected by their families precisely because of their identities."

He continued, citing research studies and program evaluations that demonstrated the effectiveness of the safe house model compared to generic youth shelters for this specific population. The data was compelling—higher rates of educational reengagement, lower rates of mental health crises, better long-term housing stability.

Senator Gonzalez, the ranking member, asked about religious freedom concerns that had been raised by some conservative organizations. "Mr. Reynolds, how would you address the argument that this legislation might force faith-based organizations to violate their religious beliefs regarding sexuality and gender identity?"

Marcus had prepared carefully for this line of questioning, knowing it represented one of the primary obstacles to the bill's passage. "Senator, this legislation does not mandate that any organization change its beliefs or practices. It simply ensures that LGBTQ+ youth have access to safe, affirming environments when they need emergency shelter and support. Faith-based organizations would remain free to operate according to their religious convictions. However, federal funding for youth homelessness services should be directed toward programs that can effectively serve all young people in need, including those who identify as LGBTQ+."

The questioning continued for nearly an hour, with committee members exploring various aspects of the legislation and the safe house model. Marcus responded with a combination of legal precision and passionate advocacy, drawing on both his professional expertise and his personal connection to the work. When appropriate, he referenced his own journey—from a corporate lawyer who had volunteered at the Tampa safe house to a full-time advocate for LGBTQ+ youth rights.

As the hearing concluded, Marcus felt cautiously optimistic. The questions had been substantive rather than performatively hostile, and several senators from both parties had expressed support for the bill's objectives, even if they had concerns about specific provisions. The legislative process would involve further negotiation and compromise, but this hearing represented a significant step forward—the first time a bill specifically addressing LGBTQ+ youth homelessness had received serious consideration at the federal level.

Outside the hearing room, Carlos was waiting with Katherine, who had flown in from Tampa to support the testimony. "You were brilliant," Katherine said, embracing Marcus warmly. "Clear, compelling, and uncompromising on the essentials while remaining diplomatic about the politics."

"It went better than I expected," Marcus admitted. "There seems to be genuine interest in addressing this issue, even from some senators I anticipated would be opposed."

"The political landscape is shifting," Carlos observed. "The data you've compiled over the years is becoming harder to ignore, and more legislators have personal connections to LGBTQ+ individuals in their families and communities."

The three colleagues made their way to a nearby café to debrief the hearing and discuss next steps. Marcus's testimony had been just one element of a broader advocacy strategy that included grassroots mobilization, media outreach, and direct engagement with key legislators and their staff.

"Senator Ramirez's aide mentioned they're still concerned about the funding mechanism," Marcus reported as they settled at a corner table. "They support the concept but want to ensure it doesn't redirect resources from other youth homelessness programs."

Katherine nodded thoughtfully. "That's a legitimate concern. We need to emphasize that this should be additional funding, not a reallocation of existing resources. All homeless youth deserve support, with specialized approaches for different populations."

Their conversation continued, analyzing the hearing's dynamics and identifying follow-up actions with specific senators whose support would be crucial for the bill's passage. Marcus was in his element, strategizing legal and political approaches with the same precision he had once applied to corporate transactions.

"How's Jayden handling your travel schedule?" Katherine asked as their discussion shifted to more personal matters. "It must be challenging for him with both his dads making frequent trips to DC."

Marcus smiled at the mention of his seven-year-old son. "He's adjusting. We try to stagger our travel so one of us is always home, and when that's not possible, he stays with my parents or yours. He's actually become quite interested in the political process—wants to know all about what happens in these hearings and how laws get made."

"Future advocate in the making," Katherine suggested with a smile.

"Possibly," Marcus agreed. "Though lately he's been talking about becoming a chef. Carlos has been teaching him to cook, and he's taking it very seriously."

The conversation turned to family updates—Katherine's children, Alex and Zoe; the safe house community back in Tampa; mutual friends and colleagues. These personal connections remained the foundation of their professional collaboration, grounding their advocacy work in relationships of mutual care and support.

After lunch, Marcus headed to a series of meetings with legislative staff members, continuing the advocacy work that would extend long beyond his formal testimony. Katherine returned to her hotel to participate in virtual meetings with safe house directors across the country, while Carlos, who had taken time off from his medical practice to support Marcus during the hearing, visited the National Gallery of Art—a favorite destination during their Washington trips.

The afternoon's meetings were productive but exhausting, requiring Marcus to explain the same concepts repeatedly while tailoring his approach to each staff member's specific concerns and priorities. By the time he returned to their hotel room in the early evening, he was mentally drained but satisfied with the day's progress.

Carlos was already there, having ordered room service dinner for them both. "I figured you wouldn't have the energy to go out," he explained, gesturing to the covered dishes. "How were the meetings?"

"Promising," Marcus replied, removing his tie and sinking gratefully into a chair. "Senator Harrington's chief of staff hinted they might co-sponsor if we can address their concerns about implementation oversight. And Congressman Lee's legislative director asked for additional data on outcomes in rural safe houses, which suggests they're seriously considering the House version of the bill."

As they ate, Marcus and Carlos discussed the day's events and the broader significance of the Safe House Protection Act for their work. Though they had different professional roles—Marcus as a legal advocate, Carlos as a physician who had developed the safe house network's health services model—their commitment to LGBTQ+ youth wellbeing united their efforts in a shared mission.

"I called Jayden earlier," Carlos mentioned. "He wanted to know if you were on TV. I explained about C-SPAN not being quite the same as regular TV."

Marcus smiled, imagining their son's confusion about the distinction. "I'll call him before bedtime. Did he seem okay?"

"He sounded great. Your mom took him to that cooking class for kids at the community center, and apparently he made something with zucchini that he's very proud of. He's saving some for us to try when we get home."

The simple domestic detail brought a wave of emotion that caught Marcus by surprise. In the midst of high-stakes advocacy work, these ordinary aspects of family life—cooking classes, bedtime calls, saved portions of zucchini dishes—anchored him in what mattered most. His work was ultimately about creating conditions where all families, in all their diverse forms, could thrive.

After dinner, Marcus called Jayden, listening to his enthusiastic description of the cooking class and assuring him that yes, making laws was important but not as exciting as cooking. The conversation restored his energy in a way that no amount of rest could have done, reminding him of the personal stakes behind his professional advocacy.

Later, as he prepared case materials for the following day's meetings, Marcus reflected on how his life had evolved since he first volunteered at the Tampa safe house. The corporate law career he had once pursued with single-minded focus now seemed like a distant memory, replaced by work that integrated his professional skills with his deepest values. The change hadn't been without sacrifices—financial security, predictable hours, conventional measures of success—but he had never regretted the path he had chosen.

His phone chimed with a text from Katherine: "Just got word that Senator Mitchell's office is drafting amendments to address the funding concerns we discussed. They want your input before tomorrow's markup session."

The message underscored the momentum building around the legislation. Marcus responded immediately, offering to review the amendments first thing in the morning. This level of engagement from the committee chair's office was a positive sign—an indication that the bill was being taken seriously rather than treated as a symbolic gesture without real legislative intent.

Carlos emerged from the bathroom, ready for bed. "More work tonight?" he asked, noting Marcus's renewed focus on his laptop.

"Just reviewing some materials for tomorrow," Marcus replied. "Senator Mitchell's office is drafting amendments to address the funding mechanism concerns. It's a good development—means they're invested in getting this right, not just going through the motions."

Carlos nodded understanding. "Just don't stay up all night. You need to be sharp tomorrow."

"I won't," Marcus promised. "One hour max, then sleep."

As Carlos settled into bed with a medical journal, Marcus continued his preparation, reviewing the legislative language with the meticulous attention to detail that had made him an effective corporate attorney and now served him well in advocacy work. Each word mattered in legislation, each provision could have far-reaching implications for the young people the safe houses served.

The next morning began early with a breakfast meeting with Katherine and representatives from allied organizations supporting the Safe House Protection Act. The coalition included LGBTQ+ rights groups, youth homelessness organizations, child welfare advocates, and mental health associations—a diverse alliance united by shared concern for vulnerable young people.

"The hearing created good momentum," Marcus reported to the group. "Now we need to maintain pressure through the markup process and ensure the bill reaches the floor with its core provisions intact."

The coalition partners discussed coordination strategies, with each organization leveraging its particular strengths and constituencies. Some would focus on grassroots mobilization, others on media outreach, still others on direct lobbying of key legislators. Marcus's role was to provide legal expertise and firsthand knowledge of the safe house model, ensuring that the legislation would effectively address the real-world challenges these programs faced.

Following the coalition meeting, Marcus headed to Senator Mitchell's office to review the proposed amendments. The senator's legislative director, a veteran Hill staffer named David Chen, greeted him warmly.

"Your testimony made an impression," Chen said as he led Marcus to a conference room. "Several senators who were on the fence have indicated they're leaning toward supporting the bill, particularly after hearing the outcomes data you presented."

"That's encouraging," Marcus replied. "The evidence really is compelling when people engage with it seriously."

They spent the next hour reviewing the amendments, which created a dedicated funding stream for LGBTQ+ youth services while maintaining support for other homeless youth programs. The language was carefully crafted to address concerns from both progressive and moderate senators, reflecting the delicate balancing act required to build a bipartisan coalition.

"These amendments strengthen the bill," Marcus concluded after thorough review. "They address the funding concerns without compromising the core protections for safe houses."

Chen seemed pleased with this assessment. "The senator is committed to getting this passed. She's been meeting one-on-one with committee members who have expressed reservations, and she believes we have the votes for committee approval. The floor vote will be more challenging, but momentum is building."

From Senator Mitchell's office, Marcus proceeded to a series of meetings with other legislative staff, allied organizations, and media representatives. Each conversation required a slightly different approach—technical legal details for policy experts, compelling narratives for communications staff, strategic considerations for coalition partners.

By mid-afternoon, Marcus was scheduled to participate in a press conference organized by the bill's sponsors. Standing on the Capitol steps alongside senators from both parties, he represented the frontline perspective of those working directly with LGBTQ+ youth experiencing homelessness.

"The Safe House Protection Act is not about politics," Marcus stated when it was his turn to speak. "It's about ensuring that every young person has access to safety, support, and opportunity, regardless of their sexual orientation or gender identity. Safe houses provide lifesaving services for youth who have often been rejected by their families and failed by other systems. This legislation would strengthen these vital programs and extend their reach to communities where no such services currently exist."

The press conference generated significant media coverage, with Marcus's comments featured in several national outlets. The bipartisan nature of the event helped frame the legislation as a common-sense approach to a serious social problem rather than a divisive cultural issue—exactly the positioning needed to build broad support.

That evening, Marcus, Carlos, and Katherine joined several key allies for dinner at a restaurant near Capitol Hill. The conversation flowed between strategic planning for the next phases of advocacy and personal updates, reflecting the relationships that had developed among these colleagues over years of shared work.

"How's the arts center coming along?" Marcus asked Desiree, who had joined the dinner via video call from Tampa. The time difference meant it was early evening for her, and she was visible in her office at the newly opened facility.

"It's exceeding all expectations," Desiree replied, her face animated even through the screen. "We've had over five hundred youth participants in the first month, and the community response has been incredible. The exhibition spaces are booked through the end of the year, and we've already had to add additional workshops to meet demand."

"That's fantastic," Marcus said sincerely. "The legal advocacy work is essential, but spaces like the arts center are where healing and growth actually happen for these young people."

The conversation continued, touching on developments across the safe house network—new programs, emerging challenges, success stories. Though Marcus's current work focused primarily on federal policy, he remained deeply connected to the day-to-day realities of safe house operations through these relationships with colleagues on the front lines.

As the dinner concluded, Katherine pulled Marcus aside for a private conversation. "I wanted to let you know that the network's board is discussing creating a new position—National Policy Director. It would formalize the advocacy work you've been doing and provide dedicated resources to expand our policy impact at both federal and state levels."

The news caught Marcus by surprise. "That sounds like a significant expansion of our approach."

"It is," Katherine confirmed. "The board recognizes that policy change is essential for creating sustainable solutions to the challenges our young people face. Your work on this legislation has demonstrated what's possible when we engage strategically at the federal level."

"I'm honored that the board is considering this direction," Marcus said thoughtfully. "It would represent a new chapter for the network—moving beyond direct service provision to more systemic change efforts."

"Exactly," Katherine agreed. "And you would be the natural choice to lead this initiative, if you're interested. It would mean transitioning from your current role to focus exclusively on policy development and advocacy."

The possibility was intriguing but also complex. Marcus had carefully balanced his legal advocacy work with ongoing involvement in direct representation of safe house residents and community education efforts. A role focused exclusively on policy would shift this balance significantly.

"I'd need to think about it," he told Katherine honestly. "There are aspects of my current work that I would miss, particularly the direct engagement with the young people we serve."

"Of course," Katherine nodded understanding. "Take all the time you need. The board is still developing the position description and funding model. I just wanted you to know it's being considered."

Back at the hotel that night, Marcus shared the conversation with Carlos, seeking his perspective on this potential career shift.

"It sounds like a natural evolution of what you've already been doing," Carlos observed. "And it would reduce your caseload, which has been overwhelming at times."

"True," Marcus acknowledged. "But I worry about becoming disconnected from the ground-level realities—the individual stories and experiences that give meaning to the policy work."

Carlos considered this thoughtfully. "That's a legitimate concern. But you could build in regular direct engagement as part of the role—maybe continuing to handle select cases or conducting regular listening sessions with safe house residents across the network."

Their discussion continued, exploring both professional considerations and personal implications. A national policy role would likely involve even more travel, which would impact their family life and Carlos's medical practice. Yet it would also create opportunities to effect change at a scale that could benefit thousands of young people beyond those directly served by the safe house network.

"Whatever you decide, I support you," Carlos assured him. "We'll figure out the logistics as a family, just as we always have."

The next morning brought the markup session for the Safe House Protection Act, where committee members would debate and vote on amendments before deciding whether to advance the bill to the full Senate. Marcus attended as an observer, sitting in the audience section while Katherine joined him for this crucial phase of the legislative process.

The session began with opening statements from committee members, revealing the range of perspectives on the legislation. Supportive senators emphasized the documented needs of LGBTQ+ youth experiencing homelessness and the proven effectiveness of the safe house model. Those with reservations raised questions about funding priorities, program evaluation metrics, and implementation oversight.

As amendments were introduced and debated, Marcus watched the legislative process unfold with both professional interest and personal investment. The technical language being discussed would have real consequences for young people like those he had represented over the years—teenagers rejected by their families, young adults aging out of foster care without support systems, transgender youth facing discrimination in traditional shelters.

One particularly contentious amendment proposed requiring parental notification for minors accessing safe house services, except in cases of documented abuse. Marcus tensed as this amendment was introduced, knowing it would effectively prevent many LGBTQ+ youth from seeking help, since family rejection often occurred without meeting legal definitions of abuse.

Senator Mitchell spoke against the amendment, citing research Marcus had provided about the specific dynamics of family rejection of LGBTQ+ youth. "In many cases, these young people leave home precisely because their parents have rejected their identity in ways that cause profound psychological harm without meeting technical definitions of abuse. Mandatory notification would create an insurmountable barrier to accessing vital services."

The amendment was ultimately defeated, though by a narrower margin than Marcus would have liked. Other amendments strengthened the bill's accountability provisions while preserving its core protections for safe houses. By the session's end, the legislation had been approved by the committee with a bipartisan vote of 13-8, clearing the way for consideration by the full Senate.

"This is a significant victory," Katherine said as they left the committee room. "Not just the vote itself, but the quality of the debate. The discussion focused on substantive policy questions rather than cultural wedge issues—a sign that the framing strategy is working."

Marcus agreed, though he remained cautious about the bill's prospects. "The floor debate will be more politically charged, and the House version faces steeper challenges. But this is definitely progress—five years ago, we couldn't even get meetings with most of these senators' offices."

The remainder of their time in Washington was filled with follow-up meetings, media interviews, and strategy sessions with allies. Throughout, Marcus maintained the careful balance he had developed over years of advocacy work—passionate about the cause while precise about the facts, firm in his principles while pragmatic about the political realities.

On their final evening before returning to Tampa, Marcus and Carlos made time for dinner alone together, away from the intensity of the legislative advocacy work. They chose a quiet restaurant in Georgetown, a favorite spot from previous visits to the capital.

"I'm proud of what you've accomplished this week," Carlos said as they settled at their table. "Regardless of whether this particular bill passes, you've advanced the conversation about LGBTQ+ youth homelessness in significant ways."

"We've advanced it," Marcus corrected gently. "Your health services model is a key part of what makes the safe house approach effective, and the data from your programs has been essential in making the case to legislators."

Carlos smiled at the acknowledgment. "We make a good team, professionally and personally."

"We do," Marcus agreed, reaching across the table to take his husband's hand. "Though I'm ready to get home to Jayden and our regular life for a while. As important as this work is, I miss our son and our community."

"Me too," Carlos admitted. "Though I suspect 'regular life' will continue to involve a fair amount of advocacy work, especially if this policy director position materializes."

Marcus nodded thoughtfully. "I'm still processing that possibility. Part of me is excited about the potential impact of focused policy work at the national level. Another part worries about losing touch with the individual stories and relationships that give meaning to the advocacy."

"You'll find the right balance," Carlos assured him. "You always have. And whatever you decide, we'll make it work as a family."

Their conversation shifted to more personal topics—Jayden's upcoming school year, plans for a family vacation, Carlos's research on LGBTQ+ health disparities, community events back in Tampa. These aspects of their life together provided essential counterbalance to the intensity of their professional commitments, grounding their advocacy work in the everyday realities of family and community.

As they walked back to their hotel through the warm summer evening, Marcus reflected on the journey that had brought him to this point—from a corporate lawyer volunteering at a local safe house to a national advocate testifying before Congress. The path had not been linear or predictable, but it had led him to work that integrated his professional skills with his deepest values, and to a life rich with meaningful relationships and purpose.

The Safe House Protection Act represented a significant milestone in this journey—the potential for federal recognition and support of a model that had begun with Elijah Johnson's vision for one safe space in Tampa. Whatever the legislation's ultimate fate, the movement it represented would continue to grow, creating spaces where LGBTQ+ youth could find safety, support, and pathways to thriving.

Back in their hotel room, Marcus received a text from Katherine: "Just heard from Senator Mitchell's office. Floor vote likely scheduled for September. We have work to do, but they're cautiously optimistic."

The message reinforced what Marcus already knew—that advocacy was a marathon, not a sprint, requiring sustained effort and strategic patience. The week's activities had been important but represented just one phase in a much longer campaign for justice and inclusion.

As he prepared for bed, Marcus found himself thinking of the young people who would be affected by this legislation—not as abstract beneficiaries of policy but as individuals with names, faces, and stories he had come to know through his work. Teenagers like Alex Johnson, who had found acceptance and support at the safe house after struggling with his transgender identity. Young adults like Adebayo, whose artistic talents had flourished once he had stable housing and mentorship. Children like Jayden, growing up in a world that was gradually becoming more accepting of diverse family structures.

These were the real measures of success—not legislative victories or professional recognition, but lives transformed through access to safety, support, and opportunity. Whatever path his career might take next, Marcus was committed to keeping these individual stories at the center of his advocacy, ensuring that policy discussions remained connected to the human realities they were meant to address.

The next morning, as they prepared to return to Tampa, Marcus received a call from Senator Mitchell herself—a rare direct contact that indicated the importance she placed on the legislation.

"Mr. Reynolds, I wanted to thank you personally for your testimony and your ongoing guidance on this bill," the senator said. "Your expertise has been invaluable in helping us craft legislation that addresses real needs while navigating the political complexities."

"Thank you, Senator," Marcus replied, genuinely appreciative of her commitment to the issue. "Your leadership on this has been crucial. It's rare to see this level of bipartisan engagement on LGBTQ+ issues."

"The data you provided made the difference," she told him. "When we can move beyond ideology to focus on documented outcomes for vulnerable young people, we find more common ground than might be expected. We still have work to do before the floor vote, but I believe we have a real chance of passing this legislation."

After the call ended, Marcus shared the conversation with Carlos and Katherine as they headed to the airport. The senator's personal engagement reinforced the sense that their advocacy efforts were gaining traction at the highest levels of government—a significant shift from the grassroots organizing that had characterized the safe house movement's early years.

On the flight home, Marcus reviewed notes from the week's meetings, identifying follow-up actions and strategic priorities for the months ahead. The policy work would continue, requiring sustained attention and effort. But for now, he was looking forward to being home—to hugging Jayden, to reconnecting with colleagues at the safe house, to sleeping in his own bed beside Carlos.

As the plane began its descent into Tampa, Marcus gazed out the window at the familiar landscape below. This city had become home in the deepest sense—not just where he lived but where he had found his true purpose and built his chosen family. The advocacy work might take him to Washington and other centers of power, but Tampa remained the heart of his personal and professional life, the community that had shaped his understanding of justice and belonging.

The safe house network was expanding nationally, and Marcus's role was evolving accordingly. But the core values that had drawn him to this work remained unchanged—the belief that every young person deserved safety, acceptance, and the opportunity to thrive; that families came in many forms, all worthy of respect and support; that legal systems should protect the vulnerable rather than reinforcing existing inequities.

These values would guide whatever decisions lay ahead, including whether to accept the National Policy Director position if it was formally offered. The title and structure of his role might change, but the fundamental purpose of his work would remain constant—creating conditions where LGBTQ+ youth could find the safety and support they needed to build lives of dignity and possibility.
